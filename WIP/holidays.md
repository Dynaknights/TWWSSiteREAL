You know what a holiday is. Most people like holidays. Here are holidays. Behold.

Earth-256-B's residents are a bit more unhinged about holidays than the average person from the 21st century because they're desperate to enjoy their lives in every conceivable way, but can you blame them?

## Beginnings and Endings

**Beginnings and Endings** <small><em>("Bon(ne) Beginnings and tender Endings to you!")</em></small> are annual holidays taking place on **January 1st** and **December 31st** respectively… You know what New Year is, dude. Think of this but cozier and disgustingly nostalgic.

Beginnings and Endings less so represents the starts and ends of years themselves and more so given 'chapters' or 'eras' of people's lives; a fresh start, so to speak.  
What is your place in the Grand Scheme of Things? Where will the story of your life take you now? Who will you meet? What morals and values will you prioritize? What adventures and trials await you? Most importantly, how will you overcome them?  
These holidays emphasize reflection on the past from perspectives and lenses that are not just your own — much like a literary work — while also promoting hope for wishes and futures, whether they be yours, another's, or the world's at large; maintaining hope in spite of all else.    
How did you get here? Did your wishes come true? Are you happy with who and where you are? Do you want to try something else?

### Practices

People love holiday decorations; this is no exception. Any structure people get their hands on can and will be outfitted with crafts (typically paper chains or crowns), incense, bells, and sappy handwritten notes.

The stereotypical Beginnings and Endings celebrations are spent with those closest to you: small house parties, feasts fitted with foods you haven't had in years (and/or sweet pastries and homemade candy), nostalgic music from your childhood, telling lighthearted stories around the table and a warm fire, creating storybooks based on your life, playing or making up games, and — of course — harassing people with ugly old pictures of themselves… or snapping new ugly photos for next year. Handwritten cards are either mailed or given to people wishing them the best in this new upcoming part in their lives, with doodles and inside jokes scrawled in the margins.  
Sometimes, these celebrations are more obnoxious: firework shows, extravagant parties with outfits themed around respective childhoods, parades, all-you-can-eat buffets, fruity alcoholic drinks, and partying from sundown to sunup. Beginnings and Endings is meant as a reflective period, but — ironically — that doesn't stop people from jumping on the opportunity to forget themselves for a little while longer.

### The Wish Dish

The most well-known Beginnings and Endings practice is the **wish dish** (sometimes called the **wish fish**); handcrafted art pieces representing hopes for the coming year or cherished memories from the last, which are sometimes kissed at midnight on January 1st for extra luck in some countries.

Wish dishes are called such as, traditionally, elaborate dioramas were made upon and adhered to bowls and plates — or circular/fish-shaped cuts of different materials — then 'presented' to others as though one were serving a fancy dinner. However, ceramic dishes fell out of style in favor of either **1)** creating gingerbread dioramas, **2)** small and compact dioramas, or — the most popular option — **3)** pop-up books with the same themes.  
… Mainly because it's difficult to store ceramic dishes that may or may not be fish-shaped with fragile sculptures glued on, hence why most of them are compact or made to be eaten.

The ideal wish dish is handcrafted with intention, care, and as many recycled (or edible) materials as possible. Those crafted out of all-fresh materials or — Stars-forbid — bought from the store are very much frowned upon, making them seen as bad omens of sorts in some places. This signals a disregard toward the world and yourself; a profound loathing that could spread to others, like an infection.

Modern wish dishes don't *have* to be shared with others and could be kept private/extremely vague, but some people do still carry a social expectation that wish dishes are made to be shared.

## Storytide

Pretty much exactly what it sounds like. **Storytide**'s a seven-day period starting from every **first of November**, which is dedicated to celebrating stories and storytelling… More than usual, anyway.

### Practices

Each day represents a different [universal iteration](/world/religion#The_Creation_Gist) — how many times the Universe has forcibly destroyed and rebuilt itself from the ground up — and the exact subject matter of each day depends on personal preference. Usually, the week is grouped together into one big celebration of stories, but some people celebrate a different genre every day, morals, characters, themes, tropes, favorite stories, religious scripture, etc.

Historically, Storytide was centered around Panpsychic scripture; it was supposed to be a dedicated time for Panpsychics to study said scripture, bounce ideas and interpretations off of each other, and build their understanding higher and higher. This is because Panpsychic scripture is… *[complicated](/world/religion/#Millions_Upon_Billions_Upon_Trillions_of_Gods!?)*. Oh so very, *very* complicated. Complicated enough that they demanded time out of their precious year to just take in how convoluted it is.  
Storytide was also seen as a time to 'stop and smell the roses,' so to speak; it was a time of gratitude, awe, and respect for the Universe's eternal strive for perfection along with examining what the Universe's Grand Scheme was intended to be. It was basically a time to chill and be at peace with nature.  
…However, it's since grown enough in popularity among non-believers that it's now more so seen as just a holiday to celebrate narratives. Along with chilling and being at peace with nature, kind of.

People do not fuck around when it comes to Storytide. Thus, schools and workplaces usually allow a week or so off, lest they face the collective wrath of literary nerds around the globe. However, schools and libraries still manage to squeeze in some Storytide-themed events, like readalouds (yes, even for the adults), book discussions, and book fairs. Authors and bookstores will typically heavily discount their prices at this time of year.  
Usual Storytides are spent in the comfort of the home by yourself or maybe with some loved ones. There are no boisterous celebrations nor parades. You just chill, write, or read books in a cozy environment while munching on your favorite snacks and chatting about it with friends and family maybe.  
People typically take this time to finally check out or finish that one book or show they've never had the time to get to, but this time is also used by many for writing sprints/marathons, blogging about their year so far, or basically speedrunning worldbuilding.
